The Untapped Frontier: Using Data to Build Healthier Workplaces

What if the data companies already collect could tell them how to help people ask for support, before it's too late?
Julián Arbini Ruy · Master's in Data Science, Big Data & Business Analytics

We talk a lot about mental health at work these days, and that's a good thing. But when I started my Master's thesis, one question kept nagging me: for all the talk, how much of it is actually measured? Companies sit on mountains of data about their people, yet the mental-health conversation still runs mostly on good intentions and gut feeling. That gap felt like an untapped frontier. So I decided to explore it.

The idea was simple to say and hard to do: take real survey data from thousands of tech workers and build a model that estimates how likely a person is to seek help for a mental-health issue, and more importantly, figure out which parts of the work environment a company can actually change to make that more likely.

Not to label individuals. That would be fragile and, frankly, a little dystopian. The goal was the opposite: insight at the level of policy, so organizations know where to put their energy.

Why this topic, and not another dataset

I could have picked something safer. But I kept coming back to how much of our lives happens at work, and how, in tech especially (with AI moving fast and that quiet background anxiety of "will I be replaced?"), well-being isn't a soft topic. It's central. And it's one where data is still barely scratching the surface. If we can use data to optimize ad clicks, surely we can use it to help people feel safe enough to ask for help.

The most valuable thing I found wasn't a number. It was where a company's influence actually lands.

The honest, messy part first

Real data is never clean, and I think that's worth saying out loud, because the polished charts you see at the end hide a lot of unglamorous work. The surveys spanned seven years, and the questions changed almost every year. "Would this hurt your career?" one year became "Has this affected your career?" the next. So I couldn't just glue the files together; I had to recognize when two differently-worded questions meant the same thing and map them concept by concept.

My favourite little detective moment: a big chunk of missing answers in the "your employer" questions turned out to belong, exactly, to self-employed people, who have no employer to answer about. Once I saw that, the fix was obvious: since my question is about what a company can do, I focused on people who actually have one.

Then came the modeling: comparing several algorithms, being careful to split the data before transforming it (to avoid the classic trap of data leakage), and judging models on more than raw accuracy. ROC-AUC cross-validation SHAP. The toolkit matters, but I'll spare you the deep end here.

The twist that made the whole thing click

Here's where it got interesting. My model predicted very well, but when I looked under the hood, almost all of that accuracy came from something obvious: whether the person already had a mental-health condition. Of course someone who's struggling is more likely to seek help. That's true, but it's not actionable. A company can't change someone's clinical history.

So I flipped the question. I zoomed in on the people a company can genuinely influence: those without a current condition, the ones still on the fence about reaching out. And there, once the "obvious" factor was out of the way, the workplace levers finally stepped into the light.

A simplified illustration based on the model's results. With an unsupportive environment the estimate sits near 11%; turn the workplace supportive and it climbs toward ~29%, roughly a 2.7× jump, driven mostly by communication, not perks.

That jump, from about 11% to 29%, is the heart of the whole thesis. For someone with a serious condition, the environment barely moves the needle; they'll seek help regardless. But for the person still deciding, the workplace can nearly triple the odds that they take the step.

The part that surprised me most

If you'd asked me at the start, I'd have bet that having benefits (an EAP, therapy coverage, a wellness budget) would be the strongest lever. It wasn't. Once I accounted for everything else, simply having benefits barely mattered. What moved people was whether mental health was actually talked about, whether they knew the resources existed, and whether they felt comfortable around their team.

The lesson for companies is almost uncomfortable in its simplicity: it's not about offering more. It's about communicating and normalizing what you already offer.

A benefit nobody knows about is a benefit that doesn't exist. A budget line item does nothing if the culture around it stays silent.

What I'm taking away from this

Two things, really. First, on the human side: the most effective thing a company can do for its people's mental health might also be the cheapest: talk about it, out loud, from the top, and make sure people know where to turn. Second, on the data side: this space is wide open. We're only beginning to point our tools at questions of well-being, and there's so much room to do it thoughtfully, with honesty about what the data can and can't say.

Because it can't say everything. My data is a snapshot, not a film; it comes from a voluntary survey, so it doesn't represent every tech worker. I talk about probabilities and associations, not certainties or causes. But being clear about those limits is exactly what makes the rest trustworthy, and it's a discipline I want to carry into everything I build.

If there's one line I'd leave you with, it's this: the companies that win at well-being won't be the ones that offer the most: they'll be the ones that talk about it the most.
